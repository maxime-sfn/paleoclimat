Water has a high thermal capacity : hence, a protracted period is required in order to bring the water in condition for freezing. That the freezing dates give a fairly accurate record of winter severity in its own vicinity is evident.

The deformation of the ice sheet itself is a complicated process and the deformation as a result of the changes in the temperature of the ice occurs almost each winter. Whenever the temperature of the ice is increased or decreased, forces will be set up rendering to either expand or contract the ice. When the temperature of ice is lowered and if the ice is free to move, linear contraction sets in at the rate of about 1/20 000 for each degree of Centigrade. If the ice is not free to move the tensile stresses will accumulate until the limit of elasticity is reached, when relief will take place. These stresses resulting from a lowering temperature are usually a maximum at the surface and decrease with the increase in temperature as the surface of the ice (where the temperature is that of unfrozen water) is approached. That is to say, the tensile stresses are greatest at the surface and decrease toward the under surface of ice sheet.

The tensile stresses in the horizontal directions accumulate until one or the other overcomes the strength of the ice and cracks form. As stated before, the tensile stresses are greatest at the surfae and rupture will begin when these stresses overcome the strength of the ice at this place. When once started, the cracks will continue either partially or entirely through the thickness of the ice.

Cracks that are once formed often open again and again, but each time they reach under surface of the ice they fill with water. The water rises about nine-tenths of the way to the surface and there solidifies. The diurnal and weekly change of the temperature during the winter months cause a sufficient expansion and contraction of the ice covering Lake Suwa to shove up the rupture into pecular ridge, known as "Omiwatari".

These ridges are of various shapes and sizes, and are discontinuous of various lenght and range from a fraction of a meter to one meter in height. They are not ordinarily an accompaniment of very small or very large lakes, although their formation is not entirely controlled by the size of the lakes, depending in the region under discussion mainly upon the climatic conditions. They are best developed during seasons when the snowfall is very light. *Fujiwhara on Five Centuries of Freezing Dates of Lake Suwa in the Central Japan, H.Arakawa*.
